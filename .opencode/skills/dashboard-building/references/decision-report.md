# Writing a decision report

Load this file when the page must explain what findings mean or support a
choice. `SKILL.md` owns the brief, evidence, and publication. This file owns the
argument and register.

## Start from the decision

Name the primary reader, the decision they hold, what they already know, and
who else may receive the report. Let reach change the treatment: a team update
can be compact; a company-wide or external recommendation needs the evidence,
uncertainty, and consequences needed to survive forwarding without narration.

Use the report register from the brief. Keep the decision and evidence legible
to a forwarded reader without turning every observation into a direct command.

## Build an answer-first pyramid

The report's first sentence is its supported answer: where things stand and the
most important implication or move. State it plainly enough to repeat in a
meeting. A useful tension or anomaly may sharpen the answer, but the evidence
beneath it must pay off the claim.

Group the support into mutually exclusive findings that together explain the
answer. Use as many as the decision needs—usually two to five—not a fixed
template. Order them by decision impact, not by research source or collection
sequence. Put secondary evidence beneath its finding rather than creating a
new peer section.

Use situation–complication–question–answer only when the reader needs the
context to understand why the decision changed. Keep it implicit when the
question is already clear; an executive reader should not wait through scene
setting for the answer.

Each finding has four distinct jobs:

1. **Claim:** the fact that changes the decision, with its strongest supported
   number or concrete example.
2. **Evidence:** the comparison, trend, or records that establish the claim,
   carrying their source, window, and material qualification.
3. **Implication:** why the claim matters to this reader and decision.
4. **Action:** the supported move, owner or first step, and expected change when
   the evidence warrants a recommendation.

Omit a job the evidence does not support. A finding without a supported remedy
ends with what is known and what must be learned before acting.

## Make headings carry the case

Use action titles: a complete sentence that states the finding and, when
supported, its implication or move. “Organic signups fell 18%; repair the two
pages that lost qualified traffic” helps a reader decide. “SEO performance”
does not.

Read the title and section headings alone. Together they should reproduce the
answer and its logic without relying on the body. Keep labels for navigation;
use claims for headings.

## Make evidence concrete

Pair important numbers with the comparison that gives them meaning. Pair an
abstract diagnosis with one representative example the reader can picture. A
number without context is trivia; an example without scale is an anecdote.

State one figure the same way everywhere. Preserve the analysis's confidence:
measured values remain measured, estimates remain labeled, and proxy evidence
uses qualified language such as “suggests” or “is consistent with.” Put a
qualification beside the claim it changes instead of collecting routine
caveats at the end.

Every visual proves one stated claim. Introduce the claim first, then let the
visual show the comparison. Use adjacent prose for cause, consequence, or
uncertainty—not to recite the marks already visible.

## Recommend proportionately

A recommendation follows from evidence, not from the expectation that every
report needs advice. State the move in concrete nouns and verbs. Include cost,
dependency, risk, or uncertainty when it changes the choice. Distinguish:

- **Do now:** evidence supports action and delay has a meaningful cost.
- **Test next:** evidence supports a hypothesis, not a causal verdict.
- **Watch:** the signal matters but has not crossed an action threshold.
- **Learn first:** missing or conflicting evidence blocks a responsible move.

End with the decisions or next moves in priority order. Detailed source
inventories or methods follow only when requested; the report ends on the
reader's choice, not the author's process.

## Check once

Before building, confirm that the first sentence answers the question; the
findings are distinct and collectively support it; headings carry the logic;
every number has one meaning; recommendations match the evidence; and a
forwarded reader can distinguish fact, inference, and open question. Fix the
argument and order before adding prose.
